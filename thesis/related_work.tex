\chapter{Related work}
\label{chap:related_work}

The problem lies at the intersection of population genetics, livestock
breed classification, genomic feature selection, representation
learning, and model interpretability. These areas developed around
different objectives, but they meet naturally when high-density SNP
profiles are used both for classification and for marker reduction.

The review focuses on the assumptions behind each family of methods
and on why a comparison under a common protocol is useful.

\section{Population genomics and SNP-based characterization}
\label{sec:pop_genetics_snps}

SNPs are among the most widely used molecular markers for describing
genomic variation within and between populations
\cite{morin2004snps}. High-throughput genotyping platforms make it
possible to measure many thousands of loci simultaneously and have
become important tools in livestock population genetics
\cite{georges2019livestock, sorbolini2016signatures}.

Population-genomic data differ from many standard machine-learning
benchmarks. The number of measured markers
can be far larger than the number of individuals, producing the
$p\gg n$ setting \cite{saeys2007feature, chafai2023mlanimal}. LD also
creates correlation among loci
\cite{slatkin2008linkage, pritchard2001linkage}, so a high-density SNP
vector should not be treated as a collection of statistically
independent features.

A classifier may exploit groups of correlated markers jointly, while a
marker-ranking method may assign relevance to one representative of a
larger correlated genomic region. Dimensionality reduction in genomics
therefore involves more than removing variables; it also requires
determining which genomic information can be discarded without losing
the population structure relevant to the task.

\section{Breed assignment and genetic traceability}
\label{sec:breed_classification_selection}

Breed assignment aims to infer the population of origin of an
individual from molecular information. It is relevant to population
characterization, conservation of livestock biodiversity, and genetic
traceability \cite{groeneveld2010diversity, dalvit2007traceability}.

A particularly relevant line of work uses multivariate discriminant
methods to derive compact genomic panels. Dimauro et al.\ applied
Canonical Discriminant Analysis (CDA) and Discriminant Analysis (DA)
to cattle, selecting SNPs that were useful for breed assignment and
traceability \cite{dimauro2013canonical}. Related work later
considered ovine populations and showed how reduced marker sets could
be used for breed and geographic assignment
\cite{dimauro2015sheep}.

These studies treat marker reduction as a supervised
population-discrimination problem: the objective is not to identify
loci associated with an arbitrary phenotype, but to preserve the
information required to distinguish predefined populations.

\section{From single-marker statistics to multivariate analysis}
\label{sec:classical_marker_discovery}

A classical way to study population differentiation is to examine
individual loci. The fixation index $F_{ST}$ measures genetic
differentiation among populations and can be estimated separately for
each marker \cite{wright1949genetical, weir1984estimating}.
Locus-wise values can therefore provide a direct and interpretable SNP
ranking.

Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) follow a related single-marker
logic, although their usual objective is association between variants
and phenotypic traits rather than breed assignment
\cite{hirschhorn2005genome}. When many loci are considered
independently, multiple testing becomes an important statistical issue
\cite{storey2003significance}.

The high-dimensional structure of genomic datasets also motivates
multivariate alternatives. Canonical discriminant approaches model
several markers jointly and construct combinations of variables that
maximize separation among predefined groups
\cite{sorbolini2016signatures}. This can be useful when information is
distributed across multiple correlated loci rather than concentrated
in a single marker.

Manca et al.\ provide another relevant example. Their multivariate
GWAS framework for cattle explicitly studied how the behavior of
genomic analysis changes as the number of available animals increases
\cite{manca2020multivariate}. The target problem in that work is
genotype--phenotype association, not breed classification, so its
numerical results are not transferred here. Its
methodological lesson is nevertheless useful: sample size can change
the relative behavior of genomic methods and should therefore be
treated as an experimental factor rather than as a fixed property of
a dataset.

\section{Machine learning for genomic prediction and feature ranking}
\label{sec:ml_genomics}

Machine-learning methods provide a different way to exploit joint
marker information. Random Forest \cite{breiman2001random}, for
example, combines multiple decision trees and can derive feature
importance measures from their predictive behavior.

Such importance measures are attractive because they produce a
marker-level ranking without requiring an independent test at every
locus. However, importance estimates can be affected by properties of
the data and the estimator, especially when predictors are correlated
\cite{strobl2007bias, nicodemus2010correlation}. Random Forest
importance is therefore best treated as a useful machine-learning
reference rather than as an unquestioned ground truth.

A neighboring research area is genomic selection, where genome-wide
markers are used to predict quantitative traits or breeding values
\cite{meuwissen2001prediction}. The focus is breed classification
rather than continuous trait prediction, but genomic selection faces
many of the same computational difficulties: many
correlated markers, limited sample sizes, and the need to generalize
beyond the available individuals
\cite{georges2019livestock, chafai2023mlanimal}.

The size of the available training population is itself an important
component of genomic learning. High-density genotyping can provide tens
of thousands of markers for each individual without necessarily
providing a comparably large number of animals. As a consequence,
genomic prediction and classification often operate in a regime in
which the dimensionality of the input is much larger than the number
of observations available for model fitting.

Increasing the number of training individuals can provide more
information about the genomic structure of the target population, but
sample size alone does not completely characterize the learning
problem. The composition of the training population also matters.
Different subsets of animals from the same breed may represent
different levels of within-breed diversity, relatedness, or uncommon
genomic profiles. A model trained on one small subset may therefore
behave differently from the same model trained on another subset of
the same size.

This observation is relevant when evaluating sample efficiency.
A single random reduction of the training population may confound the
effect of the number of available animals with the particular
individuals that happened to be selected. Repeated subsampling provides
a way to examine whether the observed behavior is stable across
different realizations of the same training-set size.

The importance of sample size has also been observed in livestock
genomic analyses. Manca et al.\ showed that the relative behavior of
genomic methods may change as the number of available animals
increases \cite{manca2020multivariate}. Although their study concerns
genotype--phenotype association rather than breed assignment, it
provides a methodological precedent for treating training-population
size as an explicit experimental variable. The same idea is transferred
here to breed classification and investigated through the controlled
sample-efficiency experiment described in
Section~\ref{sec:sample_size_protocol}.


\section{Deep genomic representation learning}
\label{sec:deep_learning_fundamentals}

Deep learning provides an alternative to working directly in the
original SNP space. Representation-learning models transform the
complete genotype vector into a new set of latent variables whose
structure is learned from data \cite{bengio2013representation}.

Autoencoders learn this transformation by compressing an observation
and reconstructing it afterwards
\cite{rumelhart1986learning, hinton2006reducing}. Variational
Autoencoders extend the idea by learning a probabilistic latent
variable and regularizing its distribution
\cite{kingma2013auto, rezende2014stochastic}.

The Variational Multi-view Genomics Predictor (VMGP) is especially
relevant here \cite{zhao2025vmgp}. It combines variational
genomic representation learning with prediction objectives. Its
original application concerns plant genomic prediction, so the
present work uses VMGP as an architectural inspiration rather than
assuming that its published results transfer directly to livestock
breed classification. Deep-learning approaches have also been reviewed
for genomic prediction in animal and plant breeding
\cite{montesinoslopez2021deep}, where the same high-dimensional and
strongly correlated marker structure motivates learned representations.

Contrastive learning follows a different principle. Instead of asking
the model to reconstruct the input, it organizes the representation by
controlling relationships between observations
\cite{oord2018representation, chen2020simple}. Different views of the
same sample can form a positive pair, while other samples act as
negatives. Normalized embeddings are often convenient in this setting
because their dot product corresponds to cosine similarity
\cite{wang2020understanding}.

The contrastive model used in this project follows the centroid N-pair
formulation of Thor and Nettelblad \cite{thor2025contrastive} rather
than the NT-Xent objective commonly associated with SimCLR. Its
architecture, augmentations, and objective are developed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:representations}.

\section{Explainable artificial intelligence for genomic features}
\label{sec:explainable_ai_paradigms}

Deep models are flexible non-linear predictors, but their internal
decisions are not immediately interpretable
\cite{guidotti2018survey, arrieta2020explainable}. Post-hoc XAI
methods attempt to estimate how input features contribute to a model
output.

SHAP \cite{lundberg2017unified} builds additive feature attributions
from Shapley-value principles \cite{shapley1953value}. Its appeal is
that it provides a common language for local feature contributions,
even though exact Shapley computation becomes combinatorial as the
number of features grows.

Integrated Gradients \cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic} uses the
differentiable structure of a neural model. Given an input
$\mathbf{x}$, a baseline $\mathbf{x}'$, and a scalar model output $F$,
the attribution of feature $i$ is

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{IG}_i(\mathbf{x})
    =
    (x_i-x'_i)
    \int_0^1
    \frac{
        \partial F\left(\mathbf{x}'+\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}')\right)
    }{
        \partial x_i
    }
    d\alpha .
    \label{eq:integrated_gradients}
\end{equation}

For genomic data, neither SHAP nor Integrated Gradients directly
produces a universal SNP panel. The methods first provide sample-level or
output-specific attributions, which are then aggregated into a global
marker ranking.
This distinction is central to Chapter~\ref{chap:feature_importance}.

\section{Positioning of this thesis}
\label{sec:research_gap}

Previous work provides strong foundations for population
differentiation, reduced-marker selection, genomic prediction,
representation learning, and model interpretation. What is less
common is to evaluate these components together under the same data
partitions, the same leakage constraints, and the same definition of
held-out generalization. In particular, marker rankings derived from
learned representations are seldom judged by the downstream performance
of the panels they produce, with preprocessing and ranking construction
controlled for leakage in the same way as the classification models.
The present thesis addresses this gap by connecting the four questions
of Section~\ref{sec:research_questions} within one evaluation
framework.

The framework is designed so that a marker-ranking method is judged
by the downstream performance of the panel it produces, not by the
scale of its importance scores, and a learned representation is judged
by the classification questions it supports, not only by the geometry
of its latent space.

The remaining chapters follow that sequence: data preparation,
representation learning, marker interpretation, and finally the
complete experimental evaluation.
