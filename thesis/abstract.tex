\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

High-density Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) arrays make it
possible to describe livestock individuals using tens of thousands of
genomic markers. This amount of information is useful for population
characterization and breed assignment, but it also creates a difficult
computational setting: the number of variables is often much larger
than the number of available animals, several markers are correlated
through linkage disequilibrium, and some breeds are represented by
comparatively few individuals.

This thesis studies whether compact genomic representations can retain
the information required for livestock breed classification and,
subsequently, whether the same representations can help identify
smaller sets of informative SNPs. Two complementary
representation-learning strategies are considered. The first is a
variational model inspired by the Variational Multi-view Genomics
Predictor (VMGP), combining genotype reconstruction with supervised
breed prediction. The second is a self-supervised hyperspherical
contrastive model that learns from different augmented views of the
same genomic profile without using breed labels in its
representation-learning objective.

The work is organized around four connected questions. First, it
investigates breed classification from high-dimensional SNP profiles.
Second, it studies how classification changes when the number of
training individuals per breed is reduced. Third, it considers how far
the genomic panel can be reduced while retaining most of its
predictive information. Finally, it examines whether marker rankings
derived from learned representations and explainability methods
provide useful reduced panels when compared with classical
population-genetics and machine-learning approaches.

The primary experimental domain is the caprine ADAPTmap dataset. A
strict separation between model development and final evaluation is
used throughout the work: preprocessing and model selection are
performed only on development data, while a locked test partition is
kept outside the development process. An independent ovine Sheep
HapMap dataset is considered as a second domain for cross-species
methodological validation. The aim of this second evaluation is not to
assume that the same loci must be informative in goats and sheep, but
to study whether the overall behavior of the computational methodology
remains consistent across two different livestock genomic settings.

Rather than treating classification, dimensionality reduction, and
marker interpretation as independent problems, the thesis connects
them within a common experimental framework. The resulting study is
intended to clarify how representation learning can be used not only
to predict breed identity, but also to reason about sample scarcity,
genomic dimensionality, and the construction of compact marker panels.
The experiments show that compact representations classify breeds with
high accuracy in two independent livestock domains, that most of that
performance is retained with ten training individuals per breed, and
that marker panels can be reduced by two orders of magnitude while
preserving the downstream classification signal. They also show that
neural feature attribution, although informative for model inspection,
does not translate automatically into a reliable marker-selection
strategy: its rankings diverge from classical and tree-based importance
and transfer less effectively to a Random Forest downstream classifier,
while robustness analyses show that attribution-selected panels become
the most marker-efficient when the panel is evaluated by the model
family they explain or by a linear classifier.
