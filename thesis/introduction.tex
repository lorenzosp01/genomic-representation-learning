\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Genomic data have become increasingly common in livestock research.
Modern genotyping arrays can measure tens of thousands of Single
Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) for a single animal, making it
possible to describe genetic variation at a scale that was not
available to earlier population studies
\cite{georges2019livestock, morin2004snps}. For Computer Science,
however, the availability of more information does not automatically
make the learning problem easier. A genomic dataset can contain many
more variables than individuals, strong correlations between nearby
markers, missing observations, and very different numbers of animals
across populations.

These characteristics make livestock genomics an interesting setting
for representation learning. Instead of asking a classifier to
operate directly on every genomic marker, one can ask whether the
original genotype profile can first be mapped to a smaller
representation that preserves the structure needed for the downstream
task. If such a representation can be learned reliably, a second
question naturally follows: which parts of the original genome are
actually responsible for the information retained by the model?

This thesis develops these two ideas together. The first part studies
how compact genomic representations can be learned and used for breed
classification. The second part moves from prediction to
interpretation, using population-genetics statistics, machine-learning
feature importance, and Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) to
construct and compare rankings of informative SNPs. The objective is
therefore not only to obtain a classifier, but to understand how
classification performance changes when either the number of training
individuals or the number of genomic markers is progressively reduced.

\section{From Genomes to Genotype Matrices}
\label{sec:from_genomes_to_matrices}

A genome is organized into chromosomes. A \emph{locus} identifies a
specific position in the genome, while a SNP refers to a
single-nucleotide variation observed at such a position. The
alternative nucleotide states observed at a locus are called
\emph{alleles}. For the autosomal loci considered in this work,
livestock individuals are diploid and therefore carry two alleles at
each position.

A convenient computational representation is the allele dosage. After
choosing a reference allele, a genotype can be encoded as

\begin{equation}
    g \in \{0,1,2\},
\end{equation}

where the value represents the number of copies of that allele.
Repeating this encoding across many SNP loci gives each individual a
high-dimensional genomic vector. Collecting the vectors of all
animals produces a genotype matrix

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P},
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the number of individuals and $P$ is the number of
markers.

In livestock datasets, $P$ can be much larger than $N$. This is the
classical $p\gg n$ situation encountered in many high-dimensional
biological learning problems
\cite{saeys2007feature, chafai2023mlanimal}. Moreover, genomic
variables are not independent. Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) creates
non-random associations between alleles at different loci, meaning
that groups of nearby markers may carry partially redundant
information \cite{slatkin2008linkage, pritchard2001linkage}.

The combination of high dimensionality, correlation, and limited
sample size is one of the central motivations for the computational
approach developed in this thesis.

\section{The Problem of Learning with Many Markers and Few Individuals}
\label{sec:high_dimensional_genomic_learning}

A straightforward breed-classification problem can be described as
learning a function that maps an individual's genotype vector to its
breed label. In practice, however, the apparent simplicity of this
formulation hides two different questions.

The first concerns \emph{sample efficiency}. If tens of thousands of
markers are available but only a small number of animals represent a
breed, how much training data is actually needed before the classifier
generalizes reliably to unseen individuals?

The second concerns \emph{marker efficiency}. A commercial
high-density SNP array may contain substantially more markers than are
required for a particular discrimination task. If a much smaller
subset preserves most of the relevant information, the computational
problem becomes easier to analyze and the selected markers may also
provide a basis for future low-density genotyping strategies
\cite{dimauro2013canonical, dimauro2015sheep}.

These questions are related but should not be confused. Reducing the
number of animals changes the amount of evidence available to estimate
both preprocessing statistics and model parameters. Reducing the
number of markers instead changes the representation available to the
classifier. The experimental framework introduced later in this
thesis therefore treats the two dimensions separately.

\section{Learning Compact Genomic Representations}
\label{sec:representation_learning}

Representation learning replaces direct reasoning in the original
feature space with a learned transformation of the data
\cite{bengio2013representation}. In the genomic setting considered
here, the goal is to map a vector containing tens of thousands of SNP
dosages to a substantially smaller latent representation while
preserving information that remains useful for breed discrimination.

Two different perspectives are explored.

The first is a variational approach inspired by the Variational
Multi-view Genomics Predictor (VMGP) \cite{zhao2025vmgp}. The model is
trained to reconstruct the original genotype profile while
simultaneously predicting breed identity. The reconstruction objective
encourages the latent variables to retain information about the input,
whereas the supervised classification objective explicitly encourages
breed-discriminative structure.

The second perspective is self-supervised. Instead of using breed
labels while the representation is learned, a contrastive model
receives different perturbed views of the same individual and learns
to organize them in a normalized, low-dimensional hyperspherical
space. Breed classification is performed only afterwards, using the
learned embeddings.

These two approaches do not make the same assumptions. The
variational model is directly guided by the target classification
task, while the contrastive model asks whether population structure
can emerge from the genomic profiles without direct breed
supervision. Their comparison is therefore useful not only as a model
benchmark, but also as a comparison between two different ways of
defining what a useful genomic representation should contain.

\section{From Prediction to Marker Interpretation}
\label{sec:black_box_xai}

A model that predicts breed identity accurately is useful, but it does
not automatically explain which markers support that prediction. This
limitation is particularly important when the objective extends
beyond classification and includes genomic marker reduction.

Classical population genetics already provides interpretable
quantities for describing differentiation between populations. The
fixation index $F_{ST}$, for example, measures differences in allele
frequencies and can be evaluated at individual loci
\cite{wright1949genetical, weir1984estimating}. Machine-learning
methods such as Random Forest can provide another type of feature
ranking based on predictive relevance \cite{breiman2001random}.

Deep models require a different form of interpretation. Post-hoc XAI
techniques estimate how strongly input variables contribute to a model
output or internal decision
\cite{guidotti2018survey, arrieta2020explainable}. Two methods
considered in this thesis are SHAP, based on Shapley-value principles
\cite{shapley1953value, lundberg2017unified}, and Integrated
Gradients, which integrates gradients along a path between a baseline
and the observed input \cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic}.

The purpose of using these methods is not to claim that a large
attribution score establishes biological causality. Instead, the
attributions are treated as computational relevance signals from
which global SNP rankings can be constructed and then tested through
reduced marker panels.

\section{Biological and Practical Motivation}
\label{sec:biological_economic_impact}

Reliable breed assignment has applications in livestock biodiversity
management, conservation of local genetic resources, population
characterization, and authentication of products of animal origin
\cite{groeneveld2010diversity, dalvit2007traceability,
      dimauro2013canonical}.

These applications motivate an analysis that goes beyond obtaining a
single high-performing classifier. In practical settings it is useful
to understand how much data are needed, whether a high-density array
is necessary for every task, and whether a compact group of markers
can preserve the relevant population information.

The work presented here should therefore be understood as a
computational investigation of these trade-offs. It does not claim to
directly produce a commercial diagnostic chip or a definitive
biological set of causal loci. Rather, it develops an experimental
framework for studying how training sample size, learned
representation, and marker-panel size interact in livestock breed
classification.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}

The thesis is organized around four research questions.

\begin{description}

    \item[\textbf{RQ1 -- Breed classification.}]
    To what extent can machine-learning and deep
    representation-learning approaches discriminate livestock breeds
    from high-dimensional SNP genotype data?

    \item[\textbf{RQ2 -- Sample efficiency.}]
    How does breed-classification performance on unseen individuals
    change as the number of training individuals per breed is reduced,
    and what is the minimum sample size required to retain reliable
    generalization performance?

    \item[\textbf{RQ3 -- Marker efficiency.}]
    What is the minimum number of SNP markers required to retain most
    of the predictive performance obtained using the complete genomic
    panel?

    \item[\textbf{RQ4 -- Marker-selection strategy.}]
    Can SNP rankings derived from learned genomic representations and
    XAI methods identify informative reduced panels when compared with
    classical population-genetics and machine-learning baselines?

\end{description}

The primary experimental domain is the caprine ADAPTmap dataset. The
same methodological framework is subsequently considered on an
independent ovine Sheep HapMap dataset. The second domain is used as a
cross-species methodological validation: the objective is to assess
whether the behavior of the pipeline remains meaningful in a different
livestock species, not to assume that the same SNP loci should be
informative in goats and sheep.

\section{Thesis Organization}
\label{sec:thesis_organization}

The remainder of the thesis follows a progressive structure.

Chapter~\ref{chap:related_work} reviews previous work on population
genomics, breed assignment, genomic dimensionality reduction,
representation learning, and model interpretability.

Chapter~\ref{chap:experimental_framework} introduces the datasets and
the experimental framework used throughout the thesis. Particular
attention is given to cohort construction, quality control, data
splitting, leakage prevention, and the distinction between development
data and the final held-out test set.

Chapter~\ref{chap:representations} develops the two learned genomic
representations. The variational and contrastive approaches are
introduced from their motivations to their concrete architectures,
objectives, and training procedures.

Chapter~\ref{chap:feature_importance} moves from representation
learning to marker interpretation. It describes how classical
statistics, machine-learning importance, and neural attributions can
be transformed into global SNP rankings and reduced genomic panels.

Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} brings the individual components
together in the experimental evaluation. Rather than presenting the
work as a sequence of isolated benchmark tables, the chapter follows
the questions that motivated the thesis: breed classification, sample
scarcity, marker reduction, marker-selection strategy, and
cross-species validation.

Finally, Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarizes the conclusions,
limitations, practical implications, and possible extensions of the
work.
